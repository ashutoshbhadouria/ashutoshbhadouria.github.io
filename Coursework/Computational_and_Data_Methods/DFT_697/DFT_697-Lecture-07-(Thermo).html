Stationary states: solutions to the TISE. From iℏ∂t∂ψ​=Hψ→Hψn​=En​ψn​, where ψn​ is a stationary state (physically observable properties are constant).

Basis sets

Basis functions are used to solve the K-S equations. A larger basis set → more accurate solutions but more computational time (check with convergence tests; consistent for all DFT codes).

The basis set size is given as an energy cutoff. For a PIB, the plane-wave (PW) energies are

EPW′s​=2mℏ2π2​((Lx​nx​​)2+(Ly​ny​​)2+(Lz​nz​​)2),nx​,ny​,nz​=1,…,∞

Choose the total plane-wave “cutoff energy” → this determines the number and type of plane waves that fall within this energy bound.

  • In 1D, for a unit cell with d∼1 nm, usually 4–6 basis functions are needed for convergence → E∼10–15 eV.
  • In 3D, for a unit cell with V∼1nm3, thousands of plane waves are needed → cutoff ≈400 eV.

Codes can be sped up with lots of tricks.

Exchange–correlation functionals

Recall the K-S equations:

[−21​∇i2​+Vext​(r)+∫∣r−r′∣ρ(r′)​dr′+δρδExc​​]ψi​=εi​ψi​

The δρδExc​​ term is where most approximations are made. Possible choices:

1) LDA — local density approximation

  • Similar to the class functionals → a function of ρ(r​) only.
  • Fit to quantum Monte Carlo calculations of the homogeneous e− gas (very expensive) ⇒ ExcLDA​=ExcLDA​[ρ] (closed form).
  • Works best for bulk materials / systems with slowly varying (with respect to space) ρ‘s.
  • Does not work well for surfaces or systems with sharp ρ gradients.

2) Generalized gradient approximation (GGA)

  • All are explicit in ρ and ∇ρ: ExcGGA​=ExcGGA​(ρ,∇ρ)
  • Standard for surfaces and interfaces.
  • Examples: PBE (Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof), RPBE (revised PBE), PW91, BEEF (Bayesian error estimation).

3) Meta-GGA

  • Incorporates second derivatives: Emeta​=Emeta​[ρ,∇ρ,∇2ρ]
  • Example: SCAN.
  • The improvement in performance is relatively small.

4) Hybrid / hyper functionals

  • Based on GGA functionals, but add a fraction of exact exchange from Hartree–Fock. HF exchange is calculated using the ψi​ from DFT:
Exchyb​=ExGGA​+a(ExHF​−ExGGA​)+EcGGA​
  • Exc​ depends on the ψi​‘s, so this leads to much longer computation times.
  • Examples: B3LYP, M06, PBE0 (a=0.25), HSE06 (hybrid screened exchange, a=0.25, with exact exchange applied over short distances).
  • Full hybrids don’t work well on metals because e− clouds screen out exchange effects.
  • Screened hybrids are better, but not great.
  • Hybrids work well for oxides (especially semiconductors).

Major limitations of existing DFT techniques

1) Band gaps (HOMO–LUMO gaps) in semiconductors or molecules

Δ≡εvalence​−εconduction​;εLUMO​−εHOMO​

Typically ΔGGA,LDA​<Δexp​, because they don’t localize e− properly (i.e. errors in e− localization).

2) Self-interaction error (SIE)

In DFT, e− feel themselves:

Exact Coulomb: U=21​i=1∑N​j=i∑​4πε0​∣ri​−rj​∣e2​DFT: 21​∬∣r​−r​′∣ρ(r​)ρ(r​′)​dr​dr​′(no j=i equivalent)

Therefore the issue is that DFT under-localizes e−; for metals the errors cancel. However, in oxides, redox processes give errors that do not cancel (as much). Most important for d and f electrons, which are highly localized.

Two practical approaches:

  1. DFT + U (Hubbard U) → an empirical term that penalizes delocalized orbitals; U depends on the material.
  2. Exact-exchange functionals: SIE is smaller because of Hartree–Fock (SIE exactly cancels in HF).

3) Van der Waals interactions

In standard GGA calculations, ρ(r) decays exponentially from the nucleus, while vdW decays as 1/r6; the exponential is faster, so intermolecular interactions are underestimated.

  • Correlated fluctuations in e− density result in vdW (sketch: two fluctuating electron clouds inducing each other).
  • In metals, this is reduced due to the presence of metallic delocalized e−.
  • vdW is essential for zeolites and hydrocarbon interactions on surfaces.

Empirical corrections:

  • (a) DFT-Dx (x = 1, 2, 3): add Ci​/r6 terms to the energy. Not great for metals (does not account for e− screening).
  • (b) Explicit non-local functionals that include vdW-like terms (→ self-consistently incorporated into energies) → OK for metals. E.g. DFT-vdW, optPBE, optB86b, BEEF-vdW.

Surface thermodynamics

Sketch: DFT + surface thermodynamics + kinetics → computational catalysis.

DFT + thermo gives:

  1. What is the state of the catalyst surface (structure, adsorbate coverage, e− properties) for given environmental conditions (T,P,V)?
  2. How can we determine adsorption isotherms for use in microkinetic models?

For surfaces, a convenient free energy is the grand potential:

Ω=Helmholtz energy (F)E−TS​​−i∑​μi​Ni​;dΩ=−SdT−PdV−i∑​Ni​dμi​

(Ω, S, V, N are extensive; T, V, μi​ are the fundamental variables.)

  • Convenient free energy because P is hard to define for a surface.
  • Also, the surface is often in quasi-equilibrium with a gas or solid bulk phase → easier to find the μi​‘s.

Consider two phases α (top) and β (bottom). If the interphase is planar: Tα=Tβ; Pα=Pβ; μiα​=μiβ​.

Ωtotal=Ωα+Ωβ+Ω​

where Ω is the excess grand potential from the interface.
